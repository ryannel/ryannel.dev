I’ve always liked maps. I like the old ones with compass roses and sea monsters, the hand-drawn
ones at the front of fantasy novels, and the road atlases my parents spread across the front of
the car on family holidays. A good map makes you want to follow a road with your finger to see
where it goes. I’ve tried making my own a few times, with tools like Inkarnate and Dungeondraft,
but never got one I was happy with.

When GPT-6 Astra came out with a new image model, I wanted another go.

Ten days, 433 turns with the agent and 300 million tokens later, I have one. It’s 12,276 by
8,184 pixels, painted in 81 overlapping pieces. It isn’t perfect. A few cities are slightly
warped, and some tiles still don’t quite line up. But it’s closer to the map in my head than
anything I’ve made before, and almost every part of it is worth zooming into.

The tricky part is size. The model that does the painting can only make a picture 1,536 pixels
wide, and I wanted one nearly eight times wider. Most of this page is about how the map gets
around that, but first it needs a look.

## Finding the look

Two models share the work. Astra plans the map, writes the prompts and checks the results. GPT
Image 2 does the painting. The first job is to settle on a style. The agent paints the same
small scene a few different ways and you pick one. Every tile after that follows your choice, so
it’s worth taking your time over it.

I spent an afternoon on this, and the early attempts looked like satellite photographs.

<Figure
  src={styleSearch}
  alt="Three early generated continents in a satellite-photograph style: snowy peaks, green lowlands and an ochre desert, with faint road lines and a title reading The Known Lands."
  caption="Continents from that first afternoon. Realistic, but with nowhere to visit."
/>

They aren’t bad. The coastlines are sharp and the mountains look real. But the roads are faint
scratches and the towns are smudges. Nothing suggests that anyone lives there, so there’s
nothing to go and look at.

So I told Astra about the things I *did* want to look at: roads and rivers, how a town sits in
the land, the feel of the place. By the evening it had painted the first version of the Lantern
Sea.

This one has individual trees. The towns have proper roofs and sit on slopes and shores. You can
follow a river from the mountains down to a sea that glows. This painting set the look for
everything that followed.

## Who lives here

A map is more fun to explore when its places have a reason to be there. So the next step is the
world itself: the peoples and their realms, where each keeps its capital and its smaller towns,
how they build, and the roads between them. All of it is planned on top of the land, so each
road leads somewhere and each port has a reason to exist.

Bring as much of this as you like. When you run out of ideas, the agent can fill the gaps. I
asked it to take inspiration from Dungeons & Dragons and to make room for all the variety in
that setting. It came back with twenty-eight realms.

## Too big for one picture

GPT Image 2 paints at most 1,536 by 1,024 pixels. The first Lantern Sea, the whole world, is one
picture that size.

The finished map is 12,276 by 8,184 pixels. That’s just over 100 million pixels, about 64 times
as many as the model can paint at once.

*Couldn’t you paint it small and scale it up?*

You could, but scaling a picture up makes it bigger without adding any detail. A town that was a
smudge becomes a larger smudge.

So the map is painted in pieces instead: a nine-by-nine grid of tiles, each one the full 1,536
by 1,024.

<Figure
  src={tileGrid}
  alt="The world map divided by a nine-by-nine grid of tiles. Four tiles in the western kingdom are highlighted in green as painted, four to their north are outlined in gold as next, and the rest are dimmed."
  caption="The plan, four tiles in. Green is painted, gold is next."
  wide
/>

Painting a single tile is straightforward. The trouble starts where two tiles meet. Paint them
separately and a river can leave one tile at a different place, width and colour from where it
enters the next.

Two things fix most of that. Each tile overlaps its neighbours by a couple of hundred pixels, so
every join is painted twice. And before the agent paints a new tile, it copies in the finished
edges of the tiles already beside it, so the model can see what it has to match.

## The first tile

Before the loop starts, one tile is painted at full size. This is the tile that tells you whether
the whole approach will work, so re-roll it until you’re happy. It’s also where you decide how
places are drawn: a handful of roofs, a marker, or something in between. Every later tile copies
what you choose here.

I went with cities as landmarks. Each city is painted into the land so it belongs there, but
drawn bigger and bolder than it would really be, so you can still pick it out from far away. You
can spot the castle from the whole-world view, then zoom in to find the town around it.

## The other eighty

From here the agent repeats the same loop for every tile. It draws a rough guide, copies in the
painted edges of any finished neighbours, and paints. Then it checks that every river and road
carries on across the joins, and repaints anything that’s broken.

Here is one tile going through that loop.

<Figure
  src={tileSteps}
  alt="Two panels. Top: a flat schematic guide with a blue river, pale roads and two numbered settlement markers, with strips of finished painting along its top and left edges. Bottom: the painted tile that came back, with the river, a bridge town, a walled fortress and forested hills."
  caption="One tile. The guide going in, and the painting that came back."
  wide
/>

The guide is deliberately crude. It has shaded relief, a blue line for the river, a pale line for
each road and a numbered marker where each settlement goes. The rock, the roofs and the folds in
the land are left for the model to invent.

Along the top and left edges of the guide are strips of real painting, copied from the tiles
already finished. They show the model the river at its true width and the light it has to match.
What came back has a bridge town, a walled fortress and forested hills along the river.

*Does it actually line up?*

Mostly. Here’s a strip across one join, at full size.

<Figure
  src={tileJoin}
  alt="A native-resolution strip across the boundary between two tiles, with the shared band marked by two dashed lines. A walled town sits in the tile above, a road runs down through the band into the tile below, and a fortress with red banners stands at the bottom right."
  caption="Shown at full size. The dashed lines mark the strip both tiles painted; the road and the fields carry straight through."
  wide
/>

Both tiles painted the band between the dashed lines. The road leaves the walled town in the
upper tile, crosses the band and carries on into the lower tile without a kink. The fields line
up too.

Because the map grows outward from where it started, most tiles have two painted sides to match,
not one. Here are four together, as they sit in the finished map.

  caption="Four tiles, from the capital down to the coast. The gold lines are the tile edges."

## Labels last

The names go on at the very end, as a separate layer over the painting. Keeping them off the
tiles means you can rename a place or move a label without repainting anything.

## Explore the world

Here’s the whole map. Drag to wander, pinch to get closer, or hold Ctrl (⌘ on a Mac) and
scroll to zoom. “Actual detail” takes you down to the houses, boats and footpaths. Turn off
“Place names” to see just the painting, and “Fit map” brings the whole world back into view.

## Make your own

Everything on this page is in a kit in my
[experiments repository](https://github.com/ryannel/experiments/tree/main/map_creation). To get
just that folder:

```sh
git clone --filter=blob:none --sparse https://github.com/ryannel/experiments.git experiments
cd experiments
git sparse-checkout set map_creation
```

Open it in Codex as its own project. It should default to GPT-6 Astra on High, which gave me the
best results. Type `$map-director` and describe the world you have in mind. The director skill
takes you through the same steps as this page: the look, the world, the first tile, the rest of
the tiles, and the labels.

It took me ten days. Now that the process is worked out, you could have one of your own by the
weekend.
