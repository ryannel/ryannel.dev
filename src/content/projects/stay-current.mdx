## Part one: the dream

### 1. How much there is to know

- Start a list of what a working engineer touches: databases, APIs, queues, caches, observability, AI. Keep going: auth, networking, containers, CI, infrastructure as code, security, frontend frameworks, browsers, mobile, accessibility, data pipelines, cost, compliance…
- Stop partway, on purpose. Any reader could add five things that are missing.
- Each item is a whole field, not one thing.
- Figure: a grid of labels that runs off the edge of the frame. Its incompleteness is the point.

### 2. Most of it holds still, some of it doesn't

- What stays put: how a B-tree finds a row, what a transaction guarantees. A fifteen-year-old textbook still teaches these correctly.
- What moves: what a tool is for. Postgres took on JSON, full-text search, geospatial queries and vectors, and the lines between kinds of database blurred. Textbooks don't cover that, and no single release note explains it.
- Speed varies by field: databases move slowly, observability quickly, and AI almost week to week.
- So readers need the state of the art as of today, with a date on it, and word when it moves.
- Figure: fields drawn side by side as timelines, with a mark for each change that alters the advice; sparse or dense depending on the field. A small strip for Postgres. Check the dates before drafting.

### 3. Some people teach this really well

- Transition: before anyone can follow a change, they need to understand the thing that's changing. That half already has great teachers.
- Lead examples: Sam Who and Bartosz Ciechanowski. One idea at a time, figures you can play with, saying what to notice.
- Passing mentions: Julia Evans, Red Blob Games, Jay Alammar, Lin Clark, the Raft visualisation, Martin Kleppmann. ByteByteGo (Alex Xu) as the counterpoint: wide reach from keeping each piece small.
- What they don't do, said plainly and not as criticism: a piece on gears never needs updating; a piece on choosing a database does. They teach how things work, not what's current or when the answer changes. Nobody does both.

### 4. The dream

- One place that does both.
- These pieces take months of research, thinking and drawing. They're a gift, and there aren't many. Most fields have none.
- Scale the teaching with AI tools: the same care and techniques across far more topics, for far more people. The reach of ByteByteGo with the depth of Ciechanowski.
- Keep it current: agents watch releases, revise the articles a change affects, and announce what moved and who it affects.
- Why now: AI is taking over implementation. To keep steering, people need to reason well about the components, so explanation matters more now, not less.

## Part two: where it stands

### 5. What's built

- Three articles, Sequences, Indexes and Storage, set the writing style and the teaching technique. Use one as the worked example: show a figure or a sandbox step and say what to notice.
- The site is live on its own domain: staycurrent.dev.

### 6. What's next

A short list in order, written as plans rather than promises:

- Cover all of databases: the first test of the approach at scale.
- Audio: a spoken version of each article for people who would rather listen. Possibly a podcast-style feed so people can subscribe and hear new articles as they land.
- Workers that keep up: on a schedule, they search for and follow the latest releases, update the articles a change affects, and publish notices and write-ups for the changes that matter.
- Expand one major area at a time until it covers everything, tying back to the list from section 1 that trailed off.

Later updates tick these off one at a time. Part one stays as it is.
