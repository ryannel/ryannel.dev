Smell isn’t really part of my world. If someone is making coffee in the morning, I don’t
notice it. If you point it out, I can usually find it, but I have to go looking. And because
I’ve never paid it much attention, I don’t have the words. I’ve never really thought about how
an orange smells different from a lemon.

That might be attention, or it might be my nose. I don’t know.

What I do know is that I like perfume, because it pulls that world out in front of me on
purpose. My wife and I buy tasting flights: small vials of five or six perfumes to try side
by side. We’ve also started playing with aroma compounds, the raw materials perfumers work with. We sit and smell them, talk about
what stands out, then look up how other people describe them and compare. It’s the same thing
we’ve done with wine and whisky for years.

The talking part is where I struggle. I can tell that two things smell different, but I don’t
have the words for how. So I wanted something to think with. A map of the possibilities, so I can go from “that’s fresh, I think”
to something more specific.

## Wheels

People have been drawing maps like this for a long time.

The best known in perfume is Michael Edwards’ Fragrance Wheel. In the early eighties Edwards
noticed that people selling perfume in department stores mostly sold the ones they liked
themselves. When a customer’s favourite was out of stock, they had no good way to suggest
something close. So he put fragrance families in a circle, with neighbours next to each other.
If you like one, try the one beside it.

Wine has its own. Ann Noble made the Wine Aroma Wheel at UC Davis in 1984, because tasters
had no shared words beyond “elegant” and “harmonious”. Her wheel starts with broad terms in the
middle, like “fruity”, and gets more specific as you move out: “citrus”, then “grapefruit”.
Plenty of versions have followed. This one is on Wikimedia Commons.

<Figure

  caption="A wine aroma wheel by Aromaster, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons. General in the middle, specific at the edge."
/>

That second idea is the one I wanted: start somewhere broad and walk outwards until you find
the word.

I started this a while ago. I looked at wheels like these and at teaching material for
perfumers, and put together a table of my own. Talking it through with an LLM, I added things
the wheels don’t usually have: a word for how each scent *feels*, and a real material you can
buy and smell to learn it. I got as far as a rough chart and stopped. I wanted a picture for
every scent, and finding or making them was taking far too long.

Notice how the photographs change as you go. The citrus band has a bit of everything:
grapefruit, orange, lemon, lime, a few tea leaves. Juicy / Sweet is mostly orange. By the
edge it’s one thing, glossy candied peel.

Tick “Hide the words” and you can see it’s one continuous photograph along each route, not a
grid of separate pictures.

*Why photographs? Wouldn’t the words do?*

For me, no. When I smell something and can’t place it, a word gives me nothing to hold on to.
A picture does. Candied orange peel looks sticky and dark and sugary, and that’s much closer to
what it smells like than the word “sweet”. The pictures give me somewhere to start.

## Making the pictures

This is the part that stopped me the first time, and the part that’s different now.

I picked it up again with GPT-6 Astra (on High) in Codex. Astra can generate images itself, so
it could do all of it: plan the wheel, write a prompt for each picture, generate it, check it,
and fit it into the layout.

Each picture has a prompt behind it. This is part of the one for the citrus family band:

> thick pink grapefruit peel curls with sharply visible bitter white pith… fresh bumpy
> yellow-green bergamot peel ribbons… thick curled candied orange peel with translucent glossy
> syrup… crushed vivid green lime peel

and then a few rules that every picture shared: fewer, larger shapes rather than a busy pile,
about half of each picture quiet so the words can sit on it, daylight, no bowls or tables or
bottles.

The parent pictures were the tricky ones. Each needs to hint at everything beneath it without
turning into a collage. The outer pictures were easier: one fruit, one texture, close up.

It took about a day. 130 pictures, each kept with its prompt and a checksum so I can trace any
of them back. The finished wheel is 9,216 pixels across, so the smallest label still reads when
you zoom in.

## It’s all on GitHub

Everything behind the wheel is in my
[experiments repository](https://github.com/ryannel/experiments/tree/main/olfactory_taxonomy),
so you can see exactly how it was made, or take it and change it:

- **The skill** Astra worked from, `olfactory-sunburst`, with its notes on layout, imagery and
  type.
- **All 130 image prompts**, one file per picture, with a record that ties each picture to its
  prompt.
- **The taxonomy as data**: every realm, family, scent, feel word and reference.
- **The viewer and its tiles**, which work offline once cloned, and the whole wheel as a single
  image.
- **The editable master**, with the full-size original photographs as a separate download.

To get just this folder:

```sh
git clone --filter=blob:none --sparse https://github.com/ryannel/experiments.git experiments
cd experiments
git sparse-checkout set olfactory_taxonomy
```

Open it in Codex and type `$olfactory-sunburst` to ask for a change: a scent you think is
missing, a better reference, a picture you’d rather see.

## What a perfumer would say

I should be honest about what this is. It’s a guide for an amateur, made for where I am now.
Perfumers aren’t trained like this, and the reasons are worth knowing.

At Givaudan’s perfumery school, students start by memorising around 500 raw materials. Every
day they’re handed one on a smelling strip and have to name it. Much of that training goes back
to Jean Carles, who set up a school in Grasse in 1946. His method lays about 60 materials out in
a grid: first you learn the contrasts between families, floral against woody, then the small
differences between materials within one family.

That broad-to-fine shape isn’t far from my wheel. What you learn is very different.

**The materials are the vocabulary, and on my wheel they’re the last ring.** A student learns
Hedione by smelling Hedione, and “Hedione” becomes the word for it. On my wheel it sits at the
very edge, after “delicate jasmine petals”. A perfumer would tell me to start at the edge and
work inwards.

**Many of the most used materials don’t look like anything.** Hedione, Iso E Super, Galaxolide,
Calone and the aldehydes are made in a lab, and there’s no object to photograph. So my wheel
dresses them up as something: Iso E Super as “smooth woody amber”, an aldehyde as soap and
candle wax. That might be roughly how they come across in a finished perfume, but it isn’t what
they smell like on a strip. Even the natural ones drift. Lemon oil doesn’t smell quite like a
cut lemon.

**The same material smells different at different strengths.** Indole is the usual example.
Strong, it smells faecal. At a trace, it’s part of what makes jasmine smell like jasmine. I’ve
put it in one slot, under “stable, manure”.

**A smell changes over time.** On a strip, citrus is gone fairly quickly while woods and musks
last for days, and a single material can smell different an hour later. Perfumers go back to the
same strip again and again. My wheel is one moment, and I left time out altogether.

**The ideas that matter most to perfumers aren’t on it.** The French perfumers’ society sorts
perfumes into seven families. Two of them, chypre and fougère, aren’t single smells but
combinations. A chypre is bright citrus over a mossy, resinous base: bergamot, labdanum,
oakmoss. A fougère is built on lavender, oakmoss and coumarin, the sweet hay smell in tonka.
All of those parts are on my wheel, scattered across three realms, with nothing to show they
belong together.

**The rest is my own judgement.** Lavender is herbal and floral, but it gets one slot. Tobacco
appears twice. A wedge is wide because it has more entries in it, not because it matters more,
and neighbours sit together because it seemed right to me, not because anyone measured it. The
feel words, like “prickly” and “rounded”, are mine. They’re not shared vocabulary.

**Noses differ.** Lots of people can’t smell certain musks at all, and most of us have
gaps we don’t know about. Which brings me back to the coffee.
