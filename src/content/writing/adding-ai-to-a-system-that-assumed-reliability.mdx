My first version was a model call behind an existing interface. It worked, briefly, and then it ran
into the fact that everything around it had been designed on the assumption that its components are
deterministic and usually right.

The assumptions that quietly broke:

- errors are exceptional rather than routine;
- the same input produces the same output;
- correctness can be checked at the boundary;
- retries are cheap and safe.

None of those held here. What I ended up doing was treating confident wrongness as a normal
operating condition and putting something in the architecture to catch it — verification,
constrained outputs, a person at a well-chosen point, and a cheap way to detect that the answer had
drifted.

<Callout type="hypothesis">
  The question I’d ask earlier next time: what does this system do when the model is confidently
  wrong, and who finds out?
</Callout>

I’ve since assumed this generalises further than I’ve actually tested. It’s one system, and the
places it bit me might be specific to how that one was built.
