I’ve always been drawn to maps. Old hand-painted ones with compass stars and sea monsters. The
hand-drawn ones on the first pages of a fantasy novel. The road atlases my parents would unfold
across the whole front of the car on family holidays. A good map makes you want to trace a road
with your finger and wonder what is at the end of it. Now and then I’ve doodled my own, using
tools like Inkarnate and Dungeondraft, chasing that feeling. There was always a world in my
head, and what ended up on the page was never it.

When GPT-6 Astra came out with its new image model, I wanted another go.

Ten days of trial and error, 433 turns with the agent and 300 million tokens later, I have one.
12,000 by 8,000 pixels, painted in 81 overlapping pieces, and every one of them is worth
zooming into.

I like it. It isn’t perfect: the cities are a little warped in places, and some of the tiles
still don’t quite line up. But it is closer to the map in my head than anything I have ever
managed to get down. Better still, you could make one of your own by the weekend.

Here’s how.

## Getting set up

Clone the `map_creation` folder of my experiments repository:

```sh
git clone --filter=blob:none --sparse https://github.com/ryannel/experiments.git experiments
cd experiments
git sparse-checkout set map_creation
```

Open it in Codex as its own project. It should default to GPT-6 Astra on High, which is what
gave me the best results. Astra does the orchestration and the review; GPT Image 2 does the
painting.

Once you’re set up, type `$map-director` and describe the world you have in mind. The director
skill will run you through a process that looks like this.

## 1. Find the look

The first thing to settle is the art style. The director paints the same small scene a few
different ways and asks you to choose, and everything painted after that will follow the same
style.

I spent an afternoon on this while trying to figure out the process. The first tries came back
looking like satellite photographs: convincing terrain, sharp coastlines, and nothing to look
at.

<Figure

  caption="An afternoon of continents. Handsome, and about as inviting as elevation data."
/>

Roads were scratches, towns were smudges, and nothing suggested anyone lived there. So I talked
to Astra about roads and rivers, about how towns should sit in the land, about the atmosphere I
was after, and by the evening it landed here.

<Figure
  src={firstMap}
  alt="The first hand-painted Lantern Sea map: two continents, Eldervane and Sarthuun, either side of a central sea, with snowy northern mountains, a desert basin in the southwest, volcanic islands in the southeast and a scattering of labelled cities."
  caption="The first Lantern Sea, painted in a single generation on the first evening. Two continents, a dozen cities, one enormous castle per people."
/>

Individual trees. Little settlements with proper roofs, tucked into slopes and shores. Rivers
you can follow and a sea that glows. This painting became the style reference for everything
that followed.

## 2. Populate the world

With the look settled, the next thing to figure out is who lives in this world. This is where
the map gets its story: the peoples and their realms, where each keeps its capital and its
lesser towns, how they build and what their country feels like, and the journeys that join
them. All of it is laid over the land, so that a road has somewhere to go and a port has a
reason to be where it is.

This is where you get to be creative. Bring as much or as little as you like, and when you
run short of ideas, ask the agent to figure it out for you.

Me? I asked it to draw inspiration from Dungeons & Dragons, and to lay out a world that made
room for all the diversity of that setting.

## 3. Paint the first tile

With the world populated, the next step is to paint one piece of it, at the size it will be
seen. This is the tile where the look and the atmosphere either come together or don’t, so
re-roll it until you are happy. It is also where you decide how places are shown. A town can be a handful of roofs, a
marker, or something in between, and whatever you choose here is what the rest of the map
will do.

I went with cities as landmarks: each one painted into the land so it looks like it belongs
there, but bigger and bolder than it should be, so it still reads from a long way out. A
castle you can find from the whole-world view, and then lean into.

## 4. Paint the map, one tile at a time

With the first tile right, the rest of the map is the same thing done eighty more times. The
world is cut into a grid of tiles, each the size the image model paints best, overlapping its
neighbours by a band a couple of hundred pixels wide. Then the loop: draw a rough guide for
the next tile from the plan, copy in the painted edges of any finished neighbours, paint,
check that every river and road runs on across the joins, and accept it or repaint the piece
that went wrong. Nothing becomes a neighbour until it has been looked at.

<Figure
  src={tileGrid}
  alt="The world map divided by a nine-by-nine grid of tiles. Four tiles in the western kingdom are highlighted in green as painted, four to their north are outlined in gold as next, and the rest are dimmed."
  caption="The plan, four tiles in. Green is painted, gold is next, and every tile shares a band with its neighbours."
  wide
/>

The guide is deliberately crude: shaded relief, a blue line for the river, a pale one for
each road, a marker where a settlement goes. Everything else, the rock and the roofs and the
folds in the land, is the model’s to invent. What is not up for invention is the strip of
finished painting copied along the top and the left. That strip is the most specific
instruction in the whole brief. It carries the river at its real width, the trees on its real
banks, and the light of the painting that already exists.

<Figure
  src={tileSteps}
  alt="Two panels. Top: a flat schematic guide with a blue river, pale roads and two numbered settlement markers, with strips of finished painting along its top and left edges. Bottom: the painted tile that came back, with the river, a bridge town, a walled fortress and forested hills."
  caption="One tile. The guide, with the finished edges of its neighbours copied in, and the painting that came back."
  wide
/>

Then look at the join, at the map’s own pixels. The two paintings overlap along the shared
band and are cut where they differ least, and the question is not whether the seam shows but
whether the water and the roads go through. A seam can be invisible and still pinch a
riverbank or double a line of trees.

<Figure
  src={tileJoin}
  alt="A native-resolution strip across the boundary between two tiles, with the shared band marked by two dashed lines. A walled town sits in the tile above, a road runs down through the band into the tile below, and a fortress with red banners stands at the bottom right."
  caption="Where a finished tile above meets a new one below. Both painted the shared band, and the road and the fields go on through it."
  wide
/>

Every tile inherits from the ones before it, so the painting spreads outward from finished
edges, and the order is chosen so that most tiles start with two painted sides rather than
one. Here are four of them together, the way they sit in the finished map.

Budget roughly one painting per tile plus a retry for every five, and expect to spend more
time looking than generating.

## 5. Labels last

The names go on at the very end, on their own layer, placed from the finished painting rather
than the plan, because the model moves a landmark a couple of hundred pixels from where it
was asked to put it more often than not. The painting underneath stays clean, so the map can
be relabelled, or lent to a different world entirely, without repainting a thing.

## What it was for

I still don’t run a campaign, and I still don’t know whose red banners fly over that fortress
in the east. But I can hang above the Lantern Sea, lean in, and keep leaning in, through the
kingdom and the coast and the river valley, down to the bridge where the road crosses. It
doesn’t run out. That was the whole point.
