I’ve always been drawn to maps. Old hand-painted ones with compass stars and sea monsters. The
hand-drawn ones at the start of a fantasy novel. The road atlases my parents would unfold
across the whole front of the car on family holidays. A good map makes you want to trace a road
with your finger and wonder what is at the end of it. Now and then I’ve doodled my own, using
tools like Inkarnate and Dungeondraft, chasing that feeling. There was always a world in my
head, and what ended up on the page was never it.

When GPT-6 Astra came out with its new image model, I wanted another go.

Ten days, 433 turns with the agent and 300 million tokens later, I have one. 12,000 by 8,000
pixels, painted in 81 overlapping pieces, and every one of them is worth zooming into.

I like it. It isn’t perfect: the cities are a little warped in places, and some of the tiles
still don’t quite line up. But it is closer to the map in my head than anything I have ever
managed to get down. Better still, you could make one of your own by the weekend.

Here’s how.

## Getting set up

Clone the `map_creation` folder of my experiments repository:

```sh
git clone --filter=blob:none --sparse https://github.com/ryannel/experiments.git experiments
cd experiments
git sparse-checkout set map_creation
```

Open it in Codex as its own project. It should default to GPT-6 Astra on High, which is what
gave me the best results. Astra does the orchestration and the review; GPT Image 2 does the
painting.

Once you’re set up, type `$map-director` and describe the world you have in mind. The
director skill runs you through a process that looks like this.

## 1. Find the look

The first thing to settle is the art style. The director paints the same small scene a few
different ways and asks you to choose, and everything painted after that will follow the same
style. When I was figuring this out I spent an afternoon on it.

<Figure
  src={styleSearch}
  alt="Three early generated continents in a satellite-photograph style: snowy peaks, green lowlands and an ochre desert, with faint road lines and a title reading The Known Lands."
  caption="An afternoon of continents. Handsome, and about as inviting as elevation data."
/>

The first tries came back looking like satellite photographs: convincing terrain, sharp
coastlines, and nothing to look at. Roads were scratches, towns were smudges, and nothing
suggested anyone lived there. So I talked to Astra about roads and rivers, about how towns
should sit in the land, about the atmosphere I was after, and by the evening it landed here.

<Figure
  src={firstMap}
  alt="The first hand-painted Lantern Sea map: two continents, Eldervane and Sarthuun, either side of a central sea, with snowy northern mountains, a desert basin in the southwest, volcanic islands in the southeast and a scattering of labelled cities."
  caption="The first Lantern Sea, painted in a single generation on the first evening. Two continents, a dozen cities, one enormous castle per people."
/>

Individual trees. Little settlements with proper roofs, tucked into slopes and shores. Rivers
you can follow and a sea that glows. This painting became the style reference for everything
that followed.

## 2. Give the world room

With a look settled, the director asks what the map is for and who lives in it, and turns
the answers into a world. Coastlines and high ground. Rivers that reach the sea. Room for
every realm to have a capital, a few lesser towns and countryside between them. The
journeys people make. You can bring a setting of your own to this. I didn’t have one.

An empty map is easy. The trouble was that I had nothing to put in it. I didn’t want to write
a setting, and I didn’t need one. I needed just enough culture and geography for the map to
have reasons: why a road goes here, why a port sits there, why the hills are dangerous.

So I borrowed. Dungeons & Dragons has spent fifty years deciding where dwarves live and what
goblins do to roads, and the 2014 Monster Manual became the baseline for who lived where and
how they behaved. Astra wrote the rest: a register of realms with capitals and secondary
towns, a note on which peoples were at war, an introduction a player might read. I have
never used any of it. It exists so that the painting has something to be about.

<Figure
  src={territorial}
  alt="The painted map overlaid with 28 numbered, colour-coded territory outlines, titled Room for countries, not just landmarks."
  caption="The allocation study. Each outline is a realm that needs a capital, lesser towns and countryside between them, rather than one giant city standing for an entire people."
/>

Once the register existed, the first map’s mistake was obvious. Every people had one city,
and it was huge. Kingdoms don’t look like that. The redesign gave each realm room for several
settlements and the wilderness between them, added a third continent, and quietly made the
map a much bigger place than a single picture could hold.

## 3. Paint one region first, at full size

Before the world, the director paints one representative region, with a river crossing, a
woodland edge and a settlement, at the size it will actually be seen. This is where the
method either works or doesn’t, and it is far cheaper to find out on one square than on
eighty. It is also where I learned the lesson the whole project rests on.

<Figure
  src={firstMapCrop}
  alt="A heavily enlarged crop of the first map around the capital Crownmere: a castle about sixty pixels wide, a river, two smaller towns and their labels, all soft and blurred."
  caption="Crownmere, the capital of the largest kingdom, at 100% zoom. This is the whole city."
/>

The map was 1536 × 1024 pixels. That is a fine picture and a hopeless atlas. The capital of
the largest kingdom was sixty pixels across. There was no leaning in, because there was
nothing further down.

Everything I tried to fix that made the map worse.

- **Re-rendering it larger.** A render at five times the pixels came back at the size I asked
  for and fell apart at 100%: dense marks that could have been anything, walls that enclosed
  nothing.
- **Placing sprites.** A hundred thousand little trees and houses drawn over the terrain was
  fast and looked flat, like a board game on top of a painting.
- **Enlarging it region by region.** Feeding crops of the map back into the model produced
  sharp new pixels around the same wrong shapes. Every malformed ship and castle was
  faithfully elaborated.

<Callout type="failed">
  All three treated the map as an image to be scaled. The information I wanted at the bottom
  of the zoom had never been painted, and no amount of processing recovers detail that was
  never there. It had to be painted at the scale it would be seen.
</Callout>

<Figure
  src={remake}
  alt="A remade Lantern Sea map with three continents and central islands, noticeably brighter and more detailed than the first, with realm names in gold lettering."
  caption="The remake at 4092 × 2728: one composition and nine detail paintings, joined along seams. Brighter, sharper, and still a long way from a roof."
/>

The first thing that worked was small. The agent painted a fresh composition of the whole
world, cut it into nine overlapping pieces, and repainted each piece as its own image. The
nine paintings were then joined along seams chosen where they differed least. It came out at
4092 × 2728, and for the first time you could see a town’s shape rather than its smudge.

Two things learnt here held for the rest of the project. The model returns 1536 × 1024
images whatever size you ask for, so that became the unit of production. And a narrow seam
through matching pixels keeps roofs and tree crowns intact where a wide blend turns them to
soup.

It was still not enough. The capital was now about a hundred and fifty pixels wide. So I did
the arithmetic the other way round: start from how much detail a single painting holds, and
work out how many paintings the world needs. The answer was a grid of nine by nine, 81
paintings, each overlapping its neighbours by a band a couple of hundred pixels wide, on a
canvas of 12,276 × 8,184. Just over 100 megapixels, and nothing upscaled.

  caption="The tile plan over the low-res world, four squares in. Green is painted, gold is next, and every square shares a band with its neighbours."

<Callout type="failed">
  The first attempt at the grid painted each square from the plan alone, without seeing its
  neighbours. Towns appeared twice: once in the tile that planned them and once in the tile
  next door that had been told they were nearby. Rivers ended in woods. A bridge stopped
  halfway across its river. Every tile looked fine on its own. Eleven tiles in, I stopped.
</Callout>

## 4. Paint the map, one square at a time

The render skill is a loop: prepare a guide for one rectangle, paint it, look at it at
native size, trace every river and road across its edges, then accept it or repair a bounded
patch with a mask. Nothing becomes a neighbour until it has been looked at. Here is that loop
in practice, and why each part of it exists.

<Figure
  src={blenderComparison}
  alt="Three panels: an earlier painted region, a flat Blender layout of the same area with corrugated grey mountains, smooth green land and roads, and the four guided paintings stitched together."
  caption="Left to right: the earlier painting, the Blender layout of the same region, and the four paintings made from it. The corrugated mountains in the middle came through as a comb."
/>

The fix for doubled towns is to paint each square from a low-resolution version of the whole
world, so that everything already has a position before anything is painted. Underneath the
atlas is a plan in world coordinates: coastlines, ridges, fourteen rivers, sixty roads and
84 named places. From that plan the agent renders a rough guide for any rectangle. Shading
suggests relief, a blue line reserves the river, flat blocks hold space for buildings.

The guide is deliberately crude, and getting it crude enough took a while. The first guides
came out of Blender, and the terrain had a ripple in it that the model dutifully painted as
comb-like mountains. Briefs that described the whole region and its neighbours produced
castles relocated from off-frame into view. Extra reference images produced extra rivers.

What worked was less. The guide became a flat 2D render that takes three seconds. The brief
leads with the atmosphere of the place, then names the handful of connections that must
hold: the river enters here, at this width; the road arrives from the west. Everything else,
the rock faces and roofs and folds in the land, is the model’s to invent.

Paint one square from the guide, then paint the next one with the finished edge of the first
copied into its guide. That is the whole method. To show it working, I’ll follow two
neighbouring regions: down from the furnaces of Cinderfast, along a small river, to the
coast at Basalt Haven.

  caption="The guide for the northern region. Almost everything that makes the finished place worth looking at is still missing, on purpose."
/>

<Figure
  src={north}
  alt="The painted Cinderfast region: large furnace buildings and quarry roads on the right, pine woodland on the left, and a winding stream descending through the middle to the bottom edge."
  caption="Cinderfast after painting and one local repair. Follow the stream from the cascades in the upper half to where it leaves at the bottom edge."

The painting turns that sparse guide into a landscape, and it adds something the plan never
asked for: a small foothill stream coming down out of the cascades. A stream is a lovely
thing to have invented. It is also a commitment, because water goes somewhere. In the first
result it didn’t. It petered out short of the bottom edge, so the agent repainted a bounded
rectangle around the last stretch and left every other pixel alone. Once the stream reached
the edge, its exit was recorded in the plan with the same authority as a river I had drawn
myself. The painting had added information back to the world.

<AtlasHandoff />

The region below starts with the last 129 pixels of the region above, copied straight into
the top of its guide. Another finished neighbour supplies the strip down the left. Those
painted strips are the most specific instruction in the whole brief. They carry the river at
its actual width, the trees on its actual banks, and the colour and scale of the painting
that already exists. The guide is suggestive. The neighbours are authoritative.

<Figure
  src={south}
  alt="The next painted region, Basalt Haven. The river enters from the woodland at the top, bends through the valley, passes under an arched road bridge and reaches an open sandy mouth. A port with copper and green roofs occupies the coast to the right."
  caption="The southern painting takes the inherited river through a road bridge to an open mouth. Basalt Haven sits along the coast to the right."
/>

Now I can follow the stream out of one painting, through the wooded valley, under the bridge
and into the sea. That whole journey is the check. Looking only at the boundary would pass a
river that joins beautifully and then stops a field short of the coast. The road gets the
same walk: in from the west, over the bridge, into the settlement, with enough water left
between the quays for a boat to get out.

The painting still changes the plan. On the eastern side of this region a separate channel
reached the coast sooner than the guide expected. Once I accepted that coastline, the brief
for the next tile east had to be rewritten, or it would have inherited an instruction to
keep painting a river through open sea.

<Figure
  src={join}
  alt="A close strip across the assembled boundary between Cinderfast and Basalt Haven, showing the stream, both banks, paths and surrounding trees continuing between the two paintings."
  caption="The assembled join at native detail. The banks, the nearby paths and the woodland all have to survive the transition."
/>

Even with the painted strip in front of it, the model reinterprets the overlap. Assembly
finds a path through the shared band where the two paintings differ least and softens it
with a six-pixel feather. Open water gets a wide, gentle blend instead, because a narrow cut
across calm sea leaves a visible step in the colour. Then I look at the seam at native size,
following the water and its banks across the join. A seam can be invisible and still pinch a
riverbank or double a tree line.

<Callout type="surprise">
  I went in assuming seams would be the hard part. They weren’t. A seam through matching
  pixels is only a compositing aid, and it will faithfully continue a mistake on either side
  of it. Every serious error in this map was a connectivity error, and every one was caught
  by tracing a route rather than by looking at a boundary.
</Callout>

The atlas grew by repeating that loop: render a guide, paint a square, trace what appeared,
join it to its neighbours, carry the reviewed edges outward. The last 61 squares were
painted in a single day. In the final batch of 36, three first attempts were thrown out and
four needed a bounded repair. The rest were accepted as painted.

Local checks sit alongside larger ones. Zoomed out, a realistic city becomes a grey smudge,
so capitals are painted with a few exaggerated landmarks rather than more roofs. A river
belongs to a journey from uplands to coast even while I’m painting one short stretch of it.

## 5. Trees and towns

Two of the skills, one for woodland and one for settlements, are what I learned about the
two things the model gets wrong most often. Trees want to be in habitats, damp hollows and
exposed slopes and managed edges, rather than scattered evenly like a census. Villages want a
reason to be where they are: a through-route, a working yard, worn ground at the doors, and
a gate that stands in an actual gap in the wall. Zoomed out, a realistic city becomes a grey
smudge, so capitals get a few exaggerated landmarks rather than more roofs. The skills carry
those rules so you don’t have to notice them the hard way.

## 6. Release

The last skill prepares the map for sharing: a clean master, the labels on their own
editable layer, safe previews, and a note of what is still unresolved. The labels go on last
and are positioned from the finished painting rather than the plan, because the model
routinely moves a landmark a couple of hundred pixels from where it was asked to put it.

Budget roughly one image call per square plus one retry in five, and expect to spend more
time looking than generating. That is the whole of it. The skills run the loop with you; you
make the choices that matter and look at every join.

## What it was for

I still don’t run a campaign, and I still don’t know who Cinderfast’s furnaces belong to. But
I can hang above the Lantern Sea, lean in, and keep leaning in, through the kingdom and the
coast and the river valley, down to the bridge where the road crosses. It doesn’t run out.
That was the whole point.
