I’ve always been drawn to maps. Old hand-painted ones with compass stars and sea monsters. The
hand-drawn ones on the first pages of a fantasy novel. The road atlases my parents would unfold
across the whole front of the car on family holidays. A good map makes you want to trace a road
with your finger and wonder what is at the end of it. Now and then I’ve doodled my own, using
tools like Inkarnate and Dungeondraft, chasing that feeling. There was always a world in my
head, and what ended up on the page was never it.

When GPT-6 Astra came out with its new image model, I wanted another go.

Ten days, 433 turns with the agent and 300 million tokens later, I have one.
12,000 by 8,000 pixels, painted in 81 overlapping pieces, and every one of them is worth
zooming into.

I like it. It isn’t perfect: the cities are a little warped in places, and some of the tiles
still don’t quite line up. But it is closer to the map in my head than anything I have ever
managed to get down. Better still, you could make one of your own by the weekend.

Here’s how.

## Get set up

Clone the `map_creation` folder of my experiments repository:

```sh
git clone --filter=blob:none --sparse https://github.com/ryannel/experiments.git experiments
cd experiments
git sparse-checkout set map_creation
```

Open it in Codex as its own project. It should default to GPT-6 Astra on High, which gave me
the best results. Astra does the orchestration and the review; GPT Image 2 does the
painting.

Once you’re set up, type `$map-director` and describe the world you have in mind. The director
skill runs you through a process like this.

## 1. Find the look

The first thing to settle is the art style. The director paints the same small scene a few
different ways and asks you to choose. Everything after follows that style.

I spent an afternoon on this. The first tries came back
looking like satellite photographs: convincing terrain, sharp coastlines, and nothing to look
at.

<Figure

  caption="An afternoon of continents. Handsome, and about as inviting as elevation data."

Roads were scratches, towns were smudges, and nothing suggested anyone lived there. So I talked
to Astra about roads and rivers, about how towns should sit in the land, about the atmosphere I
was after, and by the evening it landed here.

Individual trees. Little settlements with proper roofs, tucked into slopes and shores. Rivers
you can follow and a sea that glows. This painting became the reference for everything that
followed, the style and the coastlines both.

## 2. Populate the world

With the look settled, the question is who lives here. This is where the map gets its story: the peoples and their realms, where each keeps its capital and its
lesser towns, how they build, and the roads that join them. All of it is laid over the land,
so a road has somewhere to go and a port has a reason to be there.

Bring as much or as little as you like, and when you run short of ideas, ask the agent to figure it out for you.

Me? I asked it to draw inspiration from Dungeons & Dragons, and to lay out a world that made
room for all the diversity of that setting.

## 3. Paint the first tile

Now paint one piece of it, at the size it will be seen. Mine was the tile with the capital in
it. This is where it either comes together or doesn’t, so re-roll until you’re happy. It is
also where you decide how places are shown. A town can be a handful of roofs, a marker, or
something in between. Whatever you choose here, every later tile copies.

I went with cities as landmarks: each painted into the land so it belongs there, but bigger
and bolder than it should be, so it still reads from a long way out. A castle you can spot
from the whole-world view, then lean into.

## 4. Paint the rest, one tile at a time

With the first tile right, the rest of the map is the same thing, done eighty more times. The
world is cut into a grid of tiles, each 1,536 by 1,024 pixels because that is what GPT Image 2
paints, and each overlapping its neighbours by a couple of hundred pixels. Then the agent
loops. It draws a rough guide for the next tile and copies in the painted edges of any
finished neighbours. It paints. It checks that every river and road carries on across the
joins, and repaints the patch that didn’t.

<Figure
  src={tileGrid}
  alt="The world map divided by a nine-by-nine grid of tiles. Four tiles in the western kingdom are highlighted in green as painted, four to their north are outlined in gold as next, and the rest are dimmed."
  caption="The plan, four tiles in. Green is painted, gold is next."
  wide
/>

The guide is deliberately crude. Shaded relief, a blue line for the river, a pale one for
each road, a marker where a settlement goes. Everything else, the rock and the roofs and the
folds in the land, is the model’s to invent. The most important part is the strip of
finished painting copied in along the top and left. It shows the model the river at its real
width and the light it has to match.

<Figure
  src={tileSteps}
  alt="Two panels. Top: a flat schematic guide with a blue river, pale roads and two numbered settlement markers, with strips of finished painting along its top and left edges. Bottom: the painted tile that came back, with the river, a bridge town, a walled fortress and forested hills."
  caption="One tile. The guide going in, and the painting that came back."
  wide
/>

Then you look at the join. Where two tiles overlap, the kit cuts them along the line where
they differ least, and the agent brings you a strip across it at full size. The question
isn’t whether the seam shows. It’s whether the river and the road carry on through it. A seam
can be invisible and still pinch a riverbank or double a row of trees.

  caption="At full size. The dashed lines mark the strip both tiles painted; the road and the fields carry straight through."

together, the way they sit in the finished map.

## 5. Labels last

The names go on last, as a separate layer over the painting. You place them from the
finished painting, not the plan, because the model will happily put a town a couple of
hundred pixels from where you asked. The painting underneath stays clean. Rename everything
later, or lift the whole thing into a different setting, and you repaint nothing.

## What you end up with

It took me a while to work the process out, but it is in place now, and a weekend is enough.
What you get is a map you can zoom into, where every tile is painted at full size and each one
has something to discover. Somewhere in it is a road you will want to trace with your finger.
