Under the price of a kettle on a shopping site there’s often a line like “Arrives in 3–5 days”. At a
small shop, adding that line is about an afternoon of work. At a big company it can take many months.

People who move from a startup to a big company notice this quickly, and they often say so: “we were
much better at this at my last place.” Usually they have a point. Big companies do get messy. But the
mess doesn’t explain all of it, and the easiest way to see why is to follow this one line all the
way through.

Say a small shop and a big company both decide to add the line on the same Monday, and we time each
of them. The timings are made up, but the shape is real.

<SlowProduct />

## The afternoon

The owner of a small shop already knows everything the line needs. They know what’s on the shelf,
what’s been put aside for someone, and how long the local post usually takes. They pick a range
that’s nearly always right, type it in, and get on with the day. If a kettle turns up a day late,
they apologise to one customer.

The shop’s clock stops on Monday afternoon. The big company’s has only just started.

## Bigger than it looks

The big company makes its own kettles and sells them online and through other shops, so it has no
single shelf to look at. It has warehouses in many countries, fed by factories and ships, and the
stock in them moves all the time. There might be plenty of kettles in one warehouse and none in the
one near the customer. Some of the stock is already promised to the shops it supplies. If the
nearest warehouse runs out, the order might come from another one, or wait for a ship.

<SlowAvailability />

So the date has to be worked out for each customer, from where the stock is right now, at the moment
they look. The company could show a range wide enough to cover every case, like “Arrives in 1–4
weeks”. That would rarely be wrong, but it tells the customer almost nothing, and they might buy the
kettle from someone who promises Thursday.

The date also has to be right nearly every time. If the shop’s date is wrong one time in fifty,
that’s an apology now and then. At a million orders, the same rate means twenty thousand customers
told the wrong date, and many of them will phone to ask where their kettle is.

And once the website has promised a date, checkout has to hold the kettle, so that two customers
aren’t promised the same one.

The website isn’t the only part of the company that needs this answer, either. Sales teams need to
know what they can offer the shops they supply. Warehouses need to know which orders have a claim on
their stock. Customer care needs to know what to tell someone whose kettle is late. Today each of
them works it out with spreadsheets, phone calls and best guesses. So what started as one line on a
page is really a system that says which stock can be promised to whom, and when it will get there.
The line is one small use of it.

Just working out that this is what’s really being asked takes about two months of meetings. Nothing
has been built yet.

<SlowClock upto={1} />

## Lots of teams

No single team knows the whole answer. The warehouse team knows what’s on its shelves. The order
team keeps the record of what’s been sold, online and to other shops. Logistics knows how long
delivery takes from each warehouse. The website team, who were asked for the line, know none of it.

All four teams have their own systems, and all of them will have to change. They also have to agree
on questions none of them has had to answer before. When does stock count as reserved? Can an online
order use stock that’s held for another shop? Which warehouses can serve which customers?

Every team already has a full plan of work, so making room for this means putting something else on
hold. A team lead can decide that for their own team, but no team lead can tell the other three what
to put on hold. So the decision goes up.

Directors meet every month and can settle a lot of decisions like this. But this one changes every
team’s plans and pushes other important work back. So it goes up again, to the senior leaders who
weigh the whole company’s plans against each other.

<SlowEscalate />

Senior leaders are deciding for a lot of teams at once, and teams that change direction too often
struggle to finish anything. So the big decisions are saved up for a planning round, often once a
quarter. The delivery system goes into that round alongside everything else every team would like to
do. Say other work wins the first two rounds, and it’s chosen at the third. That’s two quarters of
waiting, and the clock is now at nearly eight months.

<SlowClock upto={2} />

## Everyone is busy

Once the delivery system is chosen, it still can’t start the next morning. None of the teams has been
sitting idle, waiting for it. The work already in their plans was chosen because it matters, and
often it really does. Some of it is half done, and some is promised to other people.

The line can only go live once every team has done its part. Suppose each team has room for new work
in nine weeks out of ten. That sounds like plenty. But for all four of our teams to have room in the
same week, each of them has to be free at once, and that happens only about two weeks in three. With
ten teams, it would be about one week in three. The more teams the work needs, the rarer those weeks
get.

<SlowReady />

Real teams don’t wait for a week when they’re all free at once. One team can start now. Another has a
release to finish first, and another has work that still comes ahead of this. The website team can
build the page with sample data while they wait. But the line can’t go live until the last team has
finished, and the more teams there are, the longer that tends to be. Here, it adds another couple of
months.

<SlowClock upto={3} />

## The same line

Meanwhile, each team changes its own systems, and then the pieces are connected and tested together.
The page might be ready long before it can show a real date, one backed by stock that checkout can
actually hold. It’s real work, and it takes a couple of months of its own.

When the day comes, adding the line to the page takes an afternoon, just as it did for the shop
owner.

<SlowClock upto={5} />

Most of that year wasn’t building. It went on working out what was being asked, waiting for a
planning round, and waiting for every team to have room. Nobody was idle in that time. Every team was
busy with work that had been judged more important, and often it was. The newcomer who says things
were better at their last place usually has a point, but even a tidy big company would take a while
to ship this.

The customer sees the same “Arrives in 3–5 days” that the shop owner typed on a Monday afternoon, a
year earlier.
