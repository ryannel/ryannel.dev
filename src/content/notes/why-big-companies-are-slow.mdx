In a small company, change can happen in an afternoon. In a big one it can take months.

That’s a common frustration for people who’ve moved from a startup to a big company: “we were
much better at this at my last place.” And they’re often right. Big companies are usually
messy, especially ones that have been around for a while. Years of growth and change leave
their mark: old systems, processes that made sense once, decisions nobody quite remembers
making.

But the mess doesn’t explain all of it. Sometimes the work itself is different. We’ll follow
one small change: showing a delivery date for a kettle, the “Arrives in 3–5 days” line you see
when you shop online.

## The small shop

For a small shop this is easy. The owner knows what’s on the shelf, what’s been put aside for
someone else, and how long the local post usually takes. For their local customers, they have
what they need to give a delivery estimate.

The owner can add the line in an afternoon.

## The big company

The big company has many factories and a complex logistics network carrying goods to its
warehouses around the world. So “do we have it?” is no longer a simple check with one place to
look. There might be plenty of kettles in one warehouse and none in the one near the customer.
Some of that stock may already be promised to online customers or retailers before it even
arrives.

The date on the page needs to change as stock moves through this network. If the local warehouse
runs out, can another one supply the order? Or will the customer have to wait for a shipment
to clear customs? This is what we want the website to be able to work out.

## Who knows the answer

The warehouse knows what’s on its shelves. The order system records online sales. The sales
team knows which stock it has allocated to retailers, and logistics knows how long delivery
from each warehouse takes.

Those teams have their own systems and ways of working. Turning what they know
into a date the website can use means agreeing how the pieces fit together. When does stock
become reserved? Can an online order use stock held for retailers? Which warehouses can serve
this customer? The website needs the answers to estimate a date, and checkout needs them to
reserve the kettle before another order claims it.

## Not worth it at first

That’s a lot of work for one line on a product page. For now, the company decides it has more
important things to build. The website puts up a rough estimate and gets by.

But the website isn’t the only thing that needs the answer. Sales teams need to know what they
can offer retailers. Warehouses need to know which orders have a claim on their stock. Customer
care needs to know what to tell someone whose kettle is late. Each of them works it out with
spreadsheets, phone calls and best guesses. Slowly the pressure grows, until someone can say
it’s worth fixing. And then it isn’t a date on a website they’re asking for, but a system that
can tell them which stock they can promise to a customer and how long it will take to get
there.

## Making the case

Even with that pressure, building it isn’t simple. Every team already has a prioritised list
of valuable work lined up. Making room for this means putting some of that work on hold.

The bigger the project, the more teams it affects and the more plans need to change. Someone
has to work out what each team would need to do, how much time and money it would take, and
what would have to wait. Getting those teams to agree on a plan takes time in itself.

Small changes can be decided within a team. This one needs time from several teams, and no
single team lead can decide what the others should put on hold. The decision goes up to senior
leaders who have the context to weigh those priorities together. They need to decide whether
the delivery system is worth delaying the work it would displace.

Those people are busy. Changing direction too often also makes it hard for teams to finish
anything, so decisions at this level might be gathered into a quarterly planning round.

If other work takes priority, it waits for the next session, or even the one after. Several
quarters can go into planning, discussion and waiting before the teams start building.

## Building it

Once the work is chosen, it takes its place in the teams’ plans. The warehouse team needs to
share available stock, the order team needs to reserve it, logistics needs to supply delivery
times, and the website needs to put those answers on the page.

That doesn’t mean every team drops what it’s doing. One can start now, another needs to finish
a release, and another has work that still comes first. Some of the delivery work can happen
in parallel. The website team can
prepare the page with sample data while the other teams build the systems it will use.

<SlowQueues />

Then the pieces have to be connected and tested together. The page may be ready long before
it can show a real delivery estimate, backed by stock that checkout can reserve. Finishing one
team’s part doesn’t finish the feature.

## The same line on the page

When the day finally comes, adding the line to the page might still take an afternoon. The
customer sees the same “Arrives in 3–5 days” the shop owner would have written. The difference
is everything behind it. The shop owner could keep the answer in their head. The big company
had to build a system that several teams feed and several more depend on.

Building that system is real work, but it isn’t where most of the months went. They went on
the work around it: working out what each team would need to do, agreeing what could wait,
waiting for a planning round, and then waiting for each team to be ready. Nobody was idle in
that time. Every team was busy with work that had been judged more important, and often it
was.

Think back to the newcomer from the start, saying “we were much better at this at my last
place.” They’re often right, and some of the difference is mess: big companies carry plenty
of it. But even a tidy one would take longer here. The change runs through teams with their
own systems, their own full plans and their own leaders to answer to, and all of them have to
make room for it at around the same time. Underneath the mess, that’s the harder problem:
getting teams that depend on each other to plan and move together.
