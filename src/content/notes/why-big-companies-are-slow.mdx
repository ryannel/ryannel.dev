Under the price of a kettle on a shopping site there’s often a line like “Arrives in 3–5 days”.
Adding that line to a page is about an afternoon of work. At a big company, it can take many months
to appear.

I want to work out where the rest of that time goes. To keep count, I’ll run a clock, and race the
big company against a small shop adding the same line.

<SlowProduct />

## The afternoon

The owner of a small shop already knows everything the line needs. They know what’s on the shelf,
what’s been put aside for someone, and how long the local post usually takes. They pick a range
that’s nearly always right, type it in, and get on with the day. If a kettle turns up a day late,
they apologise to one customer.

The big company sells the same kettle and wants the same line. Its clock starts when someone asks
for it.

<SlowClock upto={0} />

## Bigger than it looks

The first surprise is that nobody can say what the line should say.

A big company doesn’t have a shelf. It has warehouses in many countries, fed by factories and ships,
and the stock in them moves all the time. There might be plenty of kettles in one warehouse and none
in the one near the customer. Some of the kettles on a ship are already promised to retailers. If the
nearest warehouse runs out, another might be able to send the order, or the customer might have to
wait for a shipment to clear customs.

<SlowAvailability />

So the date has to be worked out for each customer, from where the stock is right now, at the moment
they look. It also has to be right far more often than the shop owner’s. At a million orders, a date
that’s wrong one time in fifty means twenty thousand late kettles, and the phone calls that go with
them.

The more the company looks, the more it finds. Checkout needs to hold the kettle, so that two
customers aren’t promised the same one. The warehouses, the sales teams and customer care turn out
to need the same answer, and each of them works it out today with spreadsheets and phone calls. What
started as one line on a page is really a system that says which stock can be promised to whom, and
when it will get there. The line is one small use of it.

Just working that out takes weeks of conversations, and nothing has been built yet.

<SlowClock upto={1} />

## Lots of teams

No single team knows the whole answer. The warehouse team knows what’s on its shelves. The order
team keeps the record of what’s been sold online. Sales knows what it has set aside for retailers,
and logistics knows how long delivery takes from each warehouse. The website team, who were asked
for the line, know none of it.

Each of these teams has its own systems, and all of them will have to change. They also have to agree
on questions none of them has had to answer before. When does stock count as reserved? Can an online
order use stock that’s held for a retailer? Which warehouses can serve which customers?

A team lead can decide to make room for something in their own team. This needs time from at
least four teams, and no team lead can tell the others what to put on hold. So the decision goes up,
to someone senior enough to weigh all of their plans against each other.

<SlowEscalate />

The people at that level are deciding for a lot of teams at once, and teams that change direction
every week never finish anything. So the big decisions tend to be saved up for a planning round,
often once a quarter. The delivery system goes into that round alongside everything else every team
would like to do. If other work wins, it waits for the next round.

<SlowClock upto={2} />

## Everyone is busy

Say the delivery system wins its round. You might expect the teams to start on it the next morning,
but none of them was sitting idle, waiting for it to arrive. Every team already has a plan full of
work, and all of that work was chosen because it mattered. Some of it is half done, and some is
promised to other people.

The line can only go live once every team has done its part, so it moves at the pace of whichever
team has room last. That gets worse quickly as the number of teams grows. Suppose each team has room
for new work in nine weeks out of ten. Two teams both have room in about eight weeks out of ten. Five
teams all have room in fewer than six. Ten teams all have room only about one week in three. Each
team is nearly always ready, and together they often aren’t.

<SlowReady />

In practice the teams don’t wait for a week when they’re all free at once. Each one starts its part
when it gets to it, and some of the work can happen side by side. The website team can build the page
with made-up data while the others build the systems it will use. The line still can’t go live until
the last team is done, so the slowest queue sets the date.

<SlowClock upto={3} />

## Building it

The building is real work. Each team changes its own systems, and then the pieces have to be
connected and tested together. The page might be ready long before it can show a real date, one
backed by stock that checkout can actually hold.

<SlowQueues />

Compared with everything before it, though, this stretch is short.

<SlowClock upto={4} />

## The tally

When the day comes, adding the line to the page takes an afternoon, just as it did for the shop
owner. The customer sees the same “Arrives in 3–5 days”.

<SlowClock upto={5} />

On the clock, building is a small part of the year. Most of it went on finding out what the question
really was, getting a decision that no single team could make, and waiting for every team to have
room. Nobody was idle in that time. Every team was busy with work that had been judged more
important, and often it was.

The shop owner could keep the answer in their head. The big company had to build something four
teams feed and several more depend on, and get all of them to make room for it at about the same
time.
