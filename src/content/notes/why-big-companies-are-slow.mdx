Under the price of a kettle on a shopping site there’s often a line like “Arrives in 3–5 days”.
Adding that line to a page is about an afternoon of work. At a big company, it can take many months
to appear.

Say a small shop and a big company both decide to add the line on the same Monday, and we keep a
clock on each of them.

<SlowProduct />

## The afternoon

The owner of a small shop already knows everything the line needs. They know what’s on the shelf,
what’s been put aside for someone, and how long the local post usually takes. They pick a range
that’s nearly always right, type it in, and get on with the day. If a kettle turns up a day late,
they apologise to one customer.

The shop’s clock stops on Monday afternoon. The big company’s has only just started.

## Bigger than it looks

A big company doesn’t have a shelf. It has warehouses in many countries, fed by factories and ships,
and the stock in them moves all the time. Some of it is already promised to retailers, and when the
nearest warehouse runs out, the order might come from another one or wait for a ship.

<SlowAvailability />

So the date has to be worked out for each customer, from where the stock is right now, at the moment
they look. A range wide enough to cover every case, like “Arrives in 1–4 weeks”, would rarely be
wrong, but it tells the customer almost nothing, and they might buy the kettle from someone who
promises Thursday.

The date also has to be right nearly every time. If the shop’s date is wrong one time in fifty,
that’s an apology now and then. At a million orders, the same rate means twenty thousand customers
told the wrong date, and the phone calls that go with them.

Checkout needs to hold the kettle, so that two customers aren’t promised the same one. The
warehouses, the sales teams and customer care turn out to need the same answer, and each of them
works it out today with spreadsheets and phone calls. What started as one line on a page is really a
system that says which stock can be promised to whom, and when it will get there. The line is one
small use of it.

In this sketch, working all that out takes about two months of conversations, and nothing has been
built yet.

<SlowClock upto={1} />

## Lots of teams

No single team knows the whole answer. The warehouse team knows what’s on its shelves. The order
team keeps the record of what’s been sold, online and to retailers. Logistics knows how long delivery
takes from each warehouse. The website team, who were asked for the line, know none of it.

All four teams have their own systems, and all of them will have to change. They also have to agree
on questions none of them has had to answer before. When does stock count as reserved? Can an online
order use stock that’s held for a retailer? Which warehouses can serve which customers?

A team lead can decide to make room for something in their own team, but no team lead can tell the
other three what to put on hold. So the decision goes up. Directors, who meet every month, can
settle a lot of these. This one changes every team’s plans, though, and pushes other important work
back, so it goes up again, to the senior leaders who weigh the whole company’s plans against each
other.

<SlowEscalate />

Those leaders are deciding for a lot of teams at once, and teams that change direction every week
never finish anything. So the big decisions are saved up for a planning round, often once a quarter.
The delivery system goes into that round alongside everything else every team would like to do. If
other work wins, it waits for the next round. By the time it’s chosen, the clock is at nearly eight
months.

<SlowClock upto={2} />

## Everyone is busy

Say the delivery system is finally chosen. You might expect the teams to start on it the next
morning, but none of them was sitting idle, waiting for it to arrive. Every team already has a plan
full of work, and all of that work was chosen because it mattered. Some of it is half done, and some
is promised to other people.

The line can only go live once every team has done its part. Suppose each team has room for new
work in nine weeks out of ten. Our four teams all have room in the same week only about two weeks in
three. Ten teams would all have room only about one week in three. Each team is nearly always ready,
and together they often aren’t.

<SlowReady />

Real teams don’t wait for a week when they’re all free at once. Each starts its part when it gets to
it, and the line goes live when the last one finishes. That softens the arithmetic but keeps its
shape: the more teams the work needs, the longer it waits for the last of them. In this sketch,
that’s another couple of months.

<SlowClock upto={3} />

## The same line

Then comes the building. Each team changes its own systems, and the pieces are connected and tested
together. It’s real work, and it takes a couple of months of its own.

When the day comes, adding the line to the page takes an afternoon, just as it did for the shop
owner.

<SlowClock upto={5} />

The customer sees the same “Arrives in 3–5 days” that the shop owner typed on a Monday afternoon,
a year earlier.
