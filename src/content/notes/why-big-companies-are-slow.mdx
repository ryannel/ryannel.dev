In a small company, a change can happen in an afternoon. In a big one it can take months.

People who move from a startup to a big company notice this quickly, and they often say so: “we were
much better at this at my last place.” Usually they have a point. Big companies do get messy,
especially ones that have been around a while. Years of growth leave old systems behind, along with
processes that made sense once and decisions nobody quite remembers making.

But the mess doesn’t explain all of it. Even a tidy big company would be slower than a small one,
because size brings some forces with it that no amount of tidying removes. None of them is anyone
being lazy or foolish. Each one is a sensible response to being big. The trouble is that they add up.

## There’s more to know

A big company simply has more going on. It sells more products, in more countries, to more kinds of
customer. Each country has its own taxes, languages, payment methods and rules. Each product has its
own suppliers and its own odd cases that someone once had to handle.

Suppose the company wants to change how it charges for delivery. In one country that’s a setting. In
thirty, it’s thirty sets of rules, some of which nobody in the room knows.

That complexity is real. It’s what the company’s customers are paying for, and it can’t be tidied
away.

## It’s spread over many teams

No one person can hold all of that, so the company splits it up. Each team owns a part, such as
checkout, stock, payments or loyalty, and each part runs on its own systems. Over time the systems
come to lean on each other. Checkout needs stock to know what can be sold. Payments needs checkout to
know what to charge.

Splitting the work makes each part manageable, but it costs something. Every pair of teams whose
systems lean on each other is a conversation that might need to happen, and the number of pairs grows
much faster than the number of teams. Ten teams make 45 possible pairs. Twenty make 190.

## So changes cut across teams

Because the systems lean on each other, a change to one often reaches others. Suppose the company
wants a new kind of discount. That sounds like a checkout change. But payments has to charge it
correctly, the loyalty programme has to decide whether it counts, and reporting has to show it to the
people who track sales. Some changes reach further still, into other business areas with their own
leaders and their own goals.

Before anyone builds anything, someone has to work out what has to change and which teams are
affected. That means reading other teams’ systems, finding the people who know them, and asking
questions they haven’t been asked before. It’s real work, and it can take weeks.

## Mistakes cost more

Every team a change reaches is one more place it can go wrong, and at this size mistakes are
expensive. When a small shop gets something wrong, it apologises to a few customers. When a company
with millions of customers gets something wrong, it can mean thousands of complaints, a letter from a
regulator, or a headline.

So big companies, sensibly, check more. A change might need sign-off from legal, from security, or
from each country it touches. Decisions that would be hard to undo get passed up to people who can see
more of the picture. Each check is there because something once went wrong without it, and each one
adds a little time.

## Everyone is already busy

All of that has to happen around work that’s already under way. Every team has a plan full of it,
and that work was chosen because it was important. If you want your change done, it has to take its
place in that order. It can’t simply jump ahead, because whatever it pushed back was also important
to someone.

Being busy costs more time than it seems to. When a team is busy most of the time, new work usually
arrives to find it in the middle of something else, so it waits. The busier the team, the longer the
wait. Suppose a piece of work needs a day from each of five teams, and the work arrives at random. If
each team is busy 80% of the time, the day of work takes about five days to get through each team on
average, so all five take about 25. At 90% busy it’s about ten days a team, or 50 in all. A team
running flat out looks efficient, but everything that passes through it waits.

## Cross-team work belongs to nobody

The order of each team’s plan is set by its own goals. That’s fair, because it’s how a team knows
whether it’s doing well. But it means work that helps several teams a little, and none of them a lot,
has no natural home.

Take the discount again. Reporting it properly would help finance a little and checkout a little, but
neither has it in their plan. Every team can see the value in it, and every team has something more
pressing of its own. So work that cuts across teams tends to wait for someone with a wider view to
pick it up.

## Someone higher up has to choose

Within one team, a team lead can decide to make room for something. A change that needs time from
several teams is different. No team lead can tell the other teams what to put on hold, so the
decision goes up to someone who can weigh those teams’ plans against each other. For a change that
reaches across business areas, that can be quite senior.

Those people are deciding for a lot of teams at once. And teams that change direction too often
struggle to finish anything, because every switch leaves half-done work behind. So leaders can’t
change their minds often. They save the big decisions up and make them on a slow cadence, perhaps
once a quarter. If other work wins this round, the change waits for the next one.

## They add up

Each of these forces on its own would slow things a little. Together they do more than that, because
each one feeds the next. The complexity is why the work is spread across teams. The spread is why
changes cut across them, and why working out what to change takes weeks. Every team a change reaches
adds a check, and a full queue to wait in. No single team owns the change, so it goes up for a
decision, and that decision comes a quarter at a time.

That’s how a change that someone could describe in a sentence can take months, sometimes most of a
year. Nobody was idle in that time. Every team was busy with work that had been judged more
important, and often it was.

## What helps

None of this makes a big company small, and I don’t think there’s one fix for it. But a few things
seem to help.

When a team owns something from end to end, it needs other teams less, so more changes stay inside
it. Teams with a little slack keep their queues short, so new work waits less. Decisions that are easy
to undo can be made lower down and sooner, which leaves the slow cadence for the few that really need
it.

And it helps just to know the forces are there. A change that touches six teams will be slow, and
it’s kinder to plan for that than to be surprised by it.

The newcomer who says things were better at their last place usually has a point. But some of what
they’re noticing is the cost of being big, and that cost is mostly people doing their jobs well.
