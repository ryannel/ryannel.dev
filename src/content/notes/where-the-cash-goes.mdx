Every so often a big company announces record sales and, in the same week, a plan to save money.
It sounds contradictory. If you’re selling more than ever, why would you be short of anything?

The answer is that a company counts its money in three different ways, and the three don’t move
together. To see how they come apart, it helps to follow one company through a year. It’s a
carmaker, and it doesn’t exist: call it Norrvik Motors. All of its numbers are made up, but they’re
roughly the right shape for a mid-sized carmaker.

## Revenue

Norrvik sells a car to a dealer for 400,000 kr. The dealer adds their own margin and sells it on,
but that part isn’t Norrvik’s money.

This year Norrvik sells 250,000 cars, so it takes in 100 billion kronor. That number is its
**revenue**: everything customers paid, or agreed to pay, before anything is taken off. It’s the
number in the headlines, and it’s a record.

## Profit

Most of that 400,000 kr is spoken for before the car leaves the factory. Here is a rough split for
one car:

- 280,000 kr for parts and materials: steel, the battery, seats, glass and a few thousand chips
- 40,000 kr to run the factory and pay the people in it
- 10,000 kr to ship the car to the dealer
- 35,000 kr towards designing future cars, marketing and running the company
- 10,000 kr set aside to repair cars under warranty

That leaves 25,000 kr, or about 6% of the price. Across 250,000 cars it comes to about 6 billion
kronor. That is Norrvik’s **profit**, before tax.

Six percent is a thin slice. If parts get 5% more expensive, that’s 14,000 kr more per car, and more
than half the profit is gone. This is why carmakers fight so hard over the price of steel and
batteries.

## Cash

So far this sounds healthy: 100 billion in, 6 billion left over. But revenue and profit are both
counted when a car is sold. They say nothing about when money actually arrives in the bank, and for
a carmaker that can be months later than when it left.

Follow one car. The parts arrive at the factory, and Norrvik pays its suppliers about a month after
that. The parts wait on a shelf, then go into a car. The car is finished, shipped across the
country or across the sea, and parked in a yard until a dealer takes it. The dealer pays a month
after that.

From paying the suppliers to being paid by the dealer is about three months. During that time the
money has gone out but hasn’t come back, so it isn’t sitting in a bank. It’s sitting in parts,
half-built cars and finished cars in a car park.

That doesn’t sound like much for one car. But Norrvik sells about 700 cars a day. Three months of
cars at different stages comes to around 63,000 cars, and Norrvik has already spent about 375,000
kr on each. That is about 23 billion kronor tied up at any one time, or nearly four years of
profit.

Accountants call this **working capital**: the money a company needs to keep its work going between
paying for things and being paid for them. It’s still Norrvik’s money. Norrvik just can’t spend it,
because it’s a car.

## Growing

Now suppose the new model sells well, and Norrvik plans to sell 25% more cars next year.

Every one of those extra cars needs its parts paid for months before the dealer pays for the car.
So the 23 billion kronor tied up has to grow by a quarter too, to about 29 billion. That extra 6
billion has to be found before the extra sales arrive, and it is almost exactly this year’s
profit.

This is the part that surprises people. A company growing quickly can be profitable on every car
and still find its bank balance falling, because each sale needs cash before it brings cash back.
The faster it grows, the more of its profit is waiting in a car park.

## Getting the cash back

Norrvik has several ways to free up that money, and each one costs something somewhere else.

**Build fewer cars ahead of demand.** If cars spend less time in yards, less money sits in them. But
then a customer who wants a blue estate waits three months instead of driving one home, and some of
them buy a different brand.

**Pay suppliers later.** If Norrvik pays after two months instead of one, a month’s worth of parts
is effectively paid for by its suppliers. Big carmakers can often insist on this. Their suppliers
tend to be smaller, though, and they now have a cash problem of their own. If one of them fails,
the production line stops for want of one part.

**Get dealers to pay sooner,** or send them more cars. A car counts as sold when the dealer takes
it, so pushing extra cars onto forecourts makes this year’s revenue look good and moves them out of
Norrvik’s yard. But the dealer still has to sell them, and if they can’t, next year they’ll order
fewer or ask for discounts.

**Discount the old stock.** When a new model is coming, last year’s cars in the yard get harder to
sell. Cutting 30,000 kr off the price turns them into cash quickly, but that’s more than the whole
25,000 kr profit on each one.

None of these is wrong. Each trades some revenue or profit for cash, or cash now for trouble later,
and which one makes sense depends on what the company is short of.

Sometimes the choice is made for it. When chips were scarce in 2021, carmakers around the world had
cars that were finished apart from a handful of chips. All the money for the steel, the battery and
the seats had been spent, and the cars couldn’t be sold until the last parts arrived.

## The new model

There is one more way profit and cash come apart, and it’s the biggest.

Suppose Norrvik decides its next car should be electric. It needs a new platform, new tooling and
perhaps a battery plant. Call it 30 billion kronor, spent over three years before the first car is
sold.

Profit doesn’t see that cost all at once. Because the factory will be used for years, the accounts
spread its cost over its working life, a slice each year, so the profit line barely moves. The bank
balance sees all of it, as it is spent.

So for three years Norrvik is paying for a factory that earns nothing yet, out of the money its
current cars make, while those cars also need more cash to grow. If the old models sell slower than
planned, or the new one is late, the cash runs out long before the profit does.

## Record sales

This is how a company can have its best year ever and still spend it worrying about cash. Revenue
says how much it sold. Profit says how much of that it keeps. Cash says whether it can pay its
suppliers on Friday, and for a carmaker a lot of it is parked outside, waiting for someone to buy a
blue estate.
