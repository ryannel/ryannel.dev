The first version of most AI features is a model call behind an existing interface. It works,
briefly, and then it runs into the fact that the surrounding system was designed on the assumption
that its components are deterministic and usually right.

The assumptions that quietly break:

- errors are exceptional rather than routine;
- the same input produces the same output;
- correctness can be checked at the boundary;
- retries are cheap and safe.

None of those hold. A system designed *around* a model treats confident wrongness as a normal
operating condition and puts something in the architecture to catch it — verification, constrained
outputs, a human at a well-chosen point, or a cheap way to detect that the answer drifted.

<Callout type="hypothesis">
  The architectural question isn’t "where do we add the model". It’s "what does this system do when
  the model is confidently wrong, and who finds out".
</Callout>

I think this is the difference between the AI features that survive their second quarter and the
ones that get quietly removed. Not enough evidence yet to say that with confidence.
