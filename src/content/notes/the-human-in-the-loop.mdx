Every company eventually has to change what it does: sell a new product, enter a new country,
comply with a new law. A small company can do that in an afternoon. A large one can take a
year, and from outside it can be hard to see why.

In this piece I’d like to look at where that time goes. We’ll grow a company one step at a time
and see what each step adds. Then I’ll talk about some of the things I see happening that could
help big companies move a bit faster and where I think AI tools can help.

## Growing

You may have bought coffee from a stall where one person takes the order, makes the drink, takes
the money and decides tomorrow’s prices. Let’s take a closer look at that business. Everything it
knows is in one head. If the owner wants to sell tea as well, they buy some tea.

The figure below draws that business. The dot is the owner and the square is the till; the line
between them is the owner using it, and the moving dots are what passes along it. Step it forward
as you read on, or let it play, and watch what each step adds.

<HitlEcosystem />

That works while the queue is short. Once it grows, one person can’t take every order, so a second
person takes orders and the first makes the drinks. The two of them now have to agree how an
order gets from one to the other. It is a small thing, a shout or a slip of paper, but it is the
first handover, and from here on any change to how orders work has to suit both of them.

As the business grows, the same happens to each job in turn. Buying beans, setting prices, paying
staff and delivering to offices each outgrow the person doing them and become a team. A team
can’t keep its job in one head either, so it writes down how the job is done, and at this size
writing it down means building a system. The shouts and slips of paper become calls between
systems.

So far every customer is in one country. Let’s add a second. It has its own tax, its own
currency, its own consumer law and its own rules about what information a company may keep on
its customers. The company doesn’t build a second set of systems. Each system it has learns a
second set of rules: the price gains a branch, and so do the receipt and the refund. A company
that sells in forty countries has forty sets of branches running through every team.

Then it starts selling through others: supermarkets that stock its beans, franchisees who run its
cafés, delivery apps and the payment companies that take the money. Each has systems of its own.
Each connection is negotiated, put in a contract, built and supported, by a team on each side.

Every one of those steps was the sensible thing to do at the time. The result is a company with
hundreds of teams, some of them whole departments, and thousands of systems, and the knowledge of
how it works is spread across all of them. The figure stops at a few hundred people and a few dozen
systems, but the shape is the same.

Now change one thing. Germany lets a customer withdraw from an online order within
fourteen days, and that reaches the order, the payment, the invoice, the refund, the returns and
the contract. Japan taxes a coffee to take away at a lower rate than one drunk in, so the price,
the till, the invoice and every franchisee have to know which it was. Almost anything worth
changing lands like this: on several systems, in several parts of the company, at once.

For anything new to reach the market and hold up under real sales volume, all of the people
behind those systems have to agree on what happens. In my experience more than half of that agreement is
not about software. It is about process, and about what the business can sell and support. The
agreeing is what makes everything slow, and anything meaningful takes tens of teams.

Models can now write much of the code, and that is where the attention has gone. The code was
never the slow part. What I have been working on is whether a model can help with the part that
is: getting those teams to agree on what to build, and keeping them coordinated while they build
it.

I think it can, and the rest of this piece is about how. I will use one small invented feature,
on a system far smaller than that company’s, so that the figures stay readable.

## The brief

Most features that cross teams start as a sentence. Ours is this:

> Show customers an estimated delivery date on the order page.

The system it lands on has a dozen services, each in its own repository and owned by its own
team. The sentence goes to all twelve. Each team reads it, works out what it means for them, and
agrees to it. Step through the teams with the arrows under the figure and watch which services
each one expects to change.

<HitlBrief />

The lit region moves. Checkout expects to calculate the date when the order is placed. The
warehouse expects to send one later, once the order has been picked. The order page expects to
show a field and for somebody else to fill it. Three teams expect no change at all. Press *Every
team at once*, and no two readings match. None of them has misread the sentence. It allows every
one of these.

For a feature that touches two teams this is fine. The two people talk, the gap shows up in the
first conversation, and a sentence is the cheapest way to start. With a dozen teams the gaps
don’t show up in conversation. They show up when the services are first connected, and each one
costs a round of rework in someone else’s code.

A longer brief would need somebody who knows what changes in each of the dozen services, down to
the fields and the calls between them. Nobody holds that much of a large system in their head.
The people who know the system best know its shape, and leave the detail to each team, which is
exactly where the different readings come from.

## The plan

A coding model can do the part nobody could. Give it the sentence and the twelve repositories and
it reads all of them and writes a plan: which service gains which field, which calls between
services change, and the order the changes have to ship in. The figure below is a sketch of the
plan it writes for the delivery date, laid out as a model lays one out, by what depends on what
and not team by team.

<HitlPlan />

The lines with a dot each settle something the sentence left open: the date is estimated by
Shipping, asked for by Checkout, stored by Orders, and revised by Shipping again once a carrier
is booked. A team that disagrees with the plan now disagrees with a line.

The plan gets us eighty or ninety percent of the way. The rest is for the teams
to find, which means the teams have to read it, and a plan this specific is long. The strip
beside the column is the whole plan, and the window on it is how much you can see at once. Now
choose *Checkout* under the figure. Six lines concern Checkout, and the thirty-nine around them
concern everyone else. To follow what happens to your own service you read past the warehouse,
the order page and payments, in whatever order the model reached them, and keep them all in mind
on the way.

My job is to understand the whole plan, across teams that aren’t mine. Each team needs to
understand the part that concerns them, and the parts next to it. Either way the piece you need
is mixed in with everything else, so a tool that lets one person check a model’s plan is the
same tool that lets twelve teams agree on it.

## The map

The plan doesn’t need to be shorter. It needs to be read one service at a time, by the people
that service belongs to, with enough around it to see where it fits.

To do that I have been building an app that first scans each repository and draws a map of the
system, TK, with the services and the calls between them. The plan is then laid onto the map
rather than written beside it.

<HitlTree />

Open the Checkout branch. Its plan assumes Shipping will answer a question Shipping has never
been asked, so click the lit line to Shipping, and click it again to cross. The one line on the
far side that answers it is marked.

That crossing is the reason for the map. The plan says Checkout will ask and Shipping will
answer; the map lets you check that both sides say so, instead of trusting the description. It
is also how the checkout team learns what Shipping does, and how the shipping team learns what
Checkout expects of it, which neither has needed to know before.

## Agreeing

The plan lives in a repository, and every change to it is a pull request. We build the plan with
the model, then review it as a group, all looking at the same map. Someone opens a service, the
team that owns it says what the plan has wrong, and the correction goes in as a pull request. We
go round until everyone is happy.

<HitlPullRequest />

The correction in the figure is invented, but it is the shape of the real ones. The model had
the warehouse send a dispatch date when an order is picked, which is what the code suggests.
Press *After*. The fix is a fact about how the warehouse runs, which the code does not say and
only the warehouse team knew.

There is no figure for the meeting itself, but it is a different meeting from the one that
started with the sentence. People argue about a field, or the order of two deployments, not
about what a sentence meant, and arguments like that come to an end. It costs TK of everyone’s
time, and when the group doesn’t converge someone still has to decide. Against that is the rework
the sentence would have caused, found later, across a dozen services.

The teams that agree the plan are the teams on call for what it builds. Whatever the model
writes, a person is paged when the delivery date is wrong, and a team that agrees a line is
agreeing to run it in production and to be the ones paged for it. That is why the plan has to be
understood in the room rather than trusted.

## Delivery

Once everyone is happy with the plan, the rest follows from it. We break it into deliverables,
open an issue for each one on its repository, and put them in the order they have to be
deployed. Each one ships behind a feature flag, so a service can deploy its part before its
neighbours have finished theirs. We agree the tests for each issue before any code is written.

Most of those tests check one service in isolation from the services around it, the large middle
layer in Spotify’s post on testing microservices. A few end-to-end tests follow the feature
across the real services, the layer that post says to keep smallest. The model writes the code
for each issue, and a test runner I have been building takes the delivery plan and wraps each
issue in its tests, so we can burn through the issues in order with the tests going green at
every step.

<HitlRunner />

Press play. The warehouse’s step is now fourth, and the tests beside each issue are the ones its
team asked for.

The model writing the code and the runner proving it both work, and both are almost beside the
point. By the time the runner starts, the hard work is done: every team involved has agreed what
they are going to build.

## The gap

I don’t expect to be building these apps for long. The models are already drawing: interactive
pages, canvases with an idea sketched out, diagrams on request. Before long I expect a model to
present a plan the way I would want to explore it, one service at a time and on a map, with no
app in between. Until then, we build them ourselves.

What I don’t expect to change is the meeting, where the people who will carry something read it,
in detail, and agree to it before it is built.
